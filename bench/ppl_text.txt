The harbour office at Velmore opens at half past six, an hour before the first ferry, and the clerk who unlocks it has done so for nineteen years. She keeps a ledger that nobody asked her to keep: the wind direction at opening, the number of gulls on the breakwater, and whether the bakery across the square has its shutters up yet. Most mornings the entries are dull. On the morning the dredger ran aground the ledger records a north-easterly breeze, eleven gulls, and shutters still down, which later turned out to matter, because the baker had seen the dredger's lights swinging oddly at four and had gone back to bed instead of telephoning anyone.

The dredger itself was an old bucket-chain vessel bought second-hand from a canal authority inland. It had been built for still water and narrow channels, and it handled the open approaches of the harbour about as gracefully as a wardrobe on a trolley. Its crew of four knew this and worked around it. They timed their runs to the slack tide, kept the bucket chain turning slowly when the swell picked up, and never, under any circumstances, let the forward anchor drag. That night the forward anchor dragged.

What follows is assembled from the inquiry, from the clerk's ledger, and from conversations with two of the crew some months afterwards.

At a quarter to four the mate noticed that the depth readings were changing faster than the chart said they should. He assumed the sensor had fouled again; it had fouled twice that week on weed carried in by the storm. He sent the youngest deckhand forward with a boat hook to clear it. The deckhand found nothing on the sensor, but he did notice that the anchor chain, which ought to have been running out ahead of the bow at a steep angle, was lying almost flat along the water and pointing towards the lighthouse. He did not understand what that meant. He mentioned it, in passing, while handing back the boat hook.

The mate understood immediately. A flat chain pointing sideways meant the anchor had broken free of the mud and the vessel was swinging on it like a gate. He called the skipper, started the main engine, and tried to bring the bow round into the wind. The engine, which had been idling for six hours in cold water, took nearly two minutes to deliver full power. By the time it did, the stern had touched the edge of the dredged channel and settled onto the sandbank that the dredger had been hired, that very month, to remove.

There was no danger to life. The tide was falling, the sea was moderate, and the vessel sat upright on soft sand with a slight list to port. The crew put out a call, made coffee, and waited. The lifeboat came out at five to stand by, mostly as a courtesy, and its coxswain later told the inquiry that he had never attended a grounding at which the crew seemed so embarrassed and so calm at the same time.

The difficulty was the timing. The morning ferry needed the channel at half past seven, and the dredger was sitting in it. The harbour master, woken at a quarter past five, made three decisions in quick succession. First, the ferry would wait at its berth until the dredger was refloated. Second, the passengers would be told the truth, not a vague story about technical problems. Third, the bakery would be asked to open early, because two hundred people waiting on a cold quay would need something warm, and he had no other way of providing it.

The baker, still feeling guilty about the lights, opened at a quarter to six and sold out of bread rolls by seven.

Refloating took longer than anyone hoped. A dredger aground on the sand it is meant to remove is in an awkward position: it cannot dig itself out, because digging beneath its own hull would only settle it deeper, and it cannot be towed off sideways without risking the bucket chain. The tug from the next harbour along arrived at twenty past seven, and its skipper spent a quarter of an hour simply looking at the problem from different angles before deciding to wait for the flood tide rather than pull against the falling one. This was, by general agreement afterwards, the right decision, and it cost the ferry company a morning's sailings.

At ten past nine the water had risen enough for the dredger to lift slightly on the swell. The tug took a line from the bow, the dredger ran its engine astern, and with a long scraping sound that the clerk recorded in her ledger as "unpleasant, like furniture being moved upstairs", the vessel slid backwards into deep water. The ferry sailed at a quarter to ten, two hours and fifteen minutes late, with most of its passengers holding paper bags from the bakery.

The inquiry found no single cause. The anchor had been laid correctly but in a patch of loose silt left by the dredger's own work the previous day. The depth sensor had been unreliable all week, so the first warning was dismissed as another fault. The engine had been left idling in a way that made a quick response impossible. And the one person who had seen something odd, the baker, had reasonably assumed that a vessel showing lights was a vessel with people awake on it who knew what they were doing.

Its recommendations were modest and sensible. Anchors would be laid outside the area dredged in the previous forty-eight hours. Depth sensors would be fitted in pairs, so that a disagreement between them would signal a fault while agreement would signal a real change. Engines on vessels working the channel at night would be kept warm enough to reach full power within thirty seconds. And the harbour office would publish a telephone number that anyone ashore could ring if they saw something on the water that worried them, with a promise that nobody would be made to feel foolish for using it.

The number was printed on a small card and pinned up in the bakery, beside the price list.

In the following winter the number was rung fourteen times. Eleven of the calls concerned nothing at all: a fishing boat working late, a reflection on the water, a buoy that had been moved for maintenance. Two concerned a yacht that had broken from its mooring and was drifting towards the rocks under the old fort; the harbour launch reached it with several minutes to spare. The last call, in February, came from a child who had been watching the sea from a bedroom window and wanted to know whether the lighthouse was supposed to flash twice or three times. It was supposed to flash twice. It had been flashing three times for an hour because of a fault in the timing relay, and the child's question was the only report anyone made. The relay was replaced the next morning.

The clerk still keeps her ledger. Asked once why she bothered, she said that most records are kept for the day something goes wrong, and that you cannot know in advance which ordinary detail will turn out to be the important one. The gulls, she admitted, have never been important. She counts them anyway.

A note on the practical side of the refloating may be useful to anyone facing a similar problem. A vessel resting on sand is held not only by its weight but by suction, which grows the longer it sits and the finer the sand. Pulling against a falling tide fights both the weight and the suction while the water that might have lifted the hull is draining away. Waiting for the flood reverses the balance: every minute adds buoyancy, and a gentle, steady pull at the right moment does more than a hard one at the wrong moment. The tug skipper's quarter of an hour spent looking was not hesitation. He was estimating the slope of the bank from the way the swell broke along it, judging where the hull was resting, and working out from the tide tables how long it would take for enough water to come back. His estimate was nine o'clock. The dredger moved at ten past.

There is also a lesson in the coffee. Crews that panic make poor decisions, and crews that are made to feel ashamed hide information. The dredger's crew reported their situation accurately and early, admitted the anchor had dragged, and answered every question from the harbour master without defending themselves. That honesty is why the harbour master could plan the morning around facts instead of guesses, and why the inquiry, months later, could describe exactly what happened rather than arguing about it.

The dredger finished its contract in the spring. The channel is deeper now than it has been in thirty years, the ferry has not been delayed by the sandbank since, and the old vessel has been sold on again, this time to a company that works a river estuary further south, where the water is calm and the mud holds an anchor well.
